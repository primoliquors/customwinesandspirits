Additive-Free Tequila, Explained

Your "100% agave" tequila can legally contain sweeteners, glycerin, oak extract, and coloring — up to 1% of the bottle, with zero disclosure on the label. Here's what additive-free actually means, the strange war over who gets to say it, and the verified brands worth your money.

The 1% rule

  1. The law allows invisible additives. Mexican regulations let tequila labeled 100% agave carry additives up to 1% of total volume with no disclosure to consumers — a rule documented in VinePair's reporting on the transparency movement.
  2. Four "mellowing agents" do most of the work. Sugar-based syrups for sweetness, glycerin for mouthfeel, oak extract for fake barrel character, and caramel color — plus dozens of other permitted flavor and aroma enhancers.
  3. The label will never tell you. Under the 1% threshold, nothing appears on the bottle. That vanilla note you taste can come from an extract rather than years in oak — and there is no way to know from the label alone.

None of this makes additive-using tequila unsafe or illegal — it makes it engineered. Additive-free is the difference between tasting the agave and tasting the recipe.

The verification war

For a few years, the tequila world had a working answer: the Tequila Matchmaker verification program, run by Grover and Scarlet Sanschagrin, physically audited distilleries — production records, chemical analysis, on-site visits — and had verified 186 products by 2020, per VinePair.

Then it fell apart. Mexico's tequila regulator, the CRT, briefly ran its own additive-free certification — then abandoned it, and in January 2024 prohibited brands from using "additive free" language at all. By March 2024 it had filed criminal complaints against the Sanschagrins, and that September it sent letters threatening product seizures over the wording, as VinePair reported. Tequila Matchmaker scrubbed its database; the successor Additive Free Alliance pulled its member list to protect brands from retaliation. In March 2025, the CRT sued the Alliance in federal court in Florida, claiming to be the sole certifier of tequila — a fight the Alliance's founder calls "David and Goliath".

Our take: whatever the legal outcome, the audits happened and the results exist. No organization actively certifies additive-free tequila today — the community's reference is the archived physical-audit list of 98 brands, last updated April 2026. That archive is what this guide, and every honest one like it, is built on. FAQ

What does additive-free tequila mean?

Tequila made from 100% blue Weber agave with nothing added after distillation — no sugar syrups, glycerin, oak extract, or caramel color, the four additives Mexican law permits up to 1% of volume without disclosure.

Why don't labels list additives?

Because under the 1% threshold the law doesn't require it. A caramel or vanilla note can come from an extract rather than barrel time, and the label will never say so.

Is there an official additive-free certification?

Not right now. The physical-audit program that verified brands was shut down under regulator pressure in 2024, and the CRT's lawsuit against the Additive Free Alliance is ongoing. The archived audit list — last updated April 2026 — is the community's working reference.

Does additive-free mean better?

It means honest: what you taste is agave, fermentation, distillation, and barrel — not a recipe. Plenty of people enjoy additive-styled tequilas; the point is knowing which one you're buying.
